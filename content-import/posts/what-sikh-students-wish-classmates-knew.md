# What Sikh Students Wish Their Classmates Knew

Most of the time, being a Sikh student on campus is just being a student. Midterms, roommate drama, the dining hall running out of the good cereal. But there are moments that are different, and they usually come down to what classmates know, or do not know, about who we are.

This is not a list of complaints. It is closer to a friendly heads-up from the Sikh students in your lecture hall, lab group, and intramural team. Every Sikh person is different, so treat this as a starting point, not a rulebook.

## The short answer

Sikh students mostly want what every student wants: to be seen as individuals. It helps when classmates ask curious, respectful questions, learn to say names correctly, avoid assumptions based on a turban or beard, and speak up if someone is being harassed. Getting to know Sikh friends is better than relying on stereotypes or the internet.

## 1. Questions are usually welcome. Tone matters.

"What does your turban mean to you?" is a great question. "Why do you wear that thing?" lands differently, even if the curiosity behind it is the same.

Asking about someone's experience, rather than demanding an explanation, makes the conversation feel like a conversation. And if your friend does not want to get into it right before a chem exam, that is fine too.

## 2. Not every Sikh looks the same

Some Sikhs wear a turban (dastaar) and keep uncut hair. Some do not. Some wear a kara, a steel bracelet, every day. Sikh women may also wear a dastaar. How someone practices is personal, and you cannot tell how "religious" someone is by looking at them. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW]

## 3. We are not who the stereotypes say we are

Sikh Americans have long dealt with being mistaken for other groups or targeted because of how they look. You do not need to be an expert on any of this. Just do not assume someone's background, beliefs, or politics from their appearance, and do not repeat jokes that treat a turban as a punchline.

## 4. Please learn our names

Names like Gurpreet, Harjot, Simran, or Jasleen are not hard. They are just unfamiliar to some people. Ask once, practice, and use the name. Shortening it into a nickname nobody asked for is not a favor. Many Sikhs also use Singh or Kaur, which carry meaning in Sikhi, so they are worth getting right too. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW]

## 5. Do not touch the turban or hair

This one is simple. Do not touch someone's turban, patka, or hair, or ask to try it on as a joke. If you are curious what it is like to wear one, many Sikh Student Associations host turban-tying events where you can learn respectfully, with people who are excited to share.

## 6. Sikhi is a whole way of life, not just a look

A lot of what Sikh students care about is not visible at all: ideas like seva (selfless service), equality, and standing up for others. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW] That is why you will often find Sikh students organizing food drives, volunteering, or cooking for crowds at a campus event. If you see a langar-style meal on campus, you are usually welcome to sit down and eat. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW]

## 7. Speaking up matters more than you think

If someone makes a comment about a Sikh classmate's turban, or worse, the most helpful thing is often simple: "Hey, not cool," or checking in afterward with "That was messed up. You okay?" You do not need a speech. You just need to not stay silent.

If something serious happens, encourage your friend to report it to campus bias response or the dean of students, and offer to go with them.

## 8. Campus spaces can be more welcoming with small changes

Small things make a difference. A quiet room for reflection or prayer. Event food that has a vegetarian option. Professors who know a major Sikh observance might fall during exam week. Students at UCSB worked through their SSA and the Muslim Student Association to open an interfaith reflection room on campus. That kind of change starts with someone asking.

## 9. We want to share, on our terms

Sikh students often love talking about their festivals, music, food, and history. Come to an SSA event. Ask your friend if you can join them at the gurdwara sometime. Try the chai. The best way to understand is to show up.

## How to be a great friend to Sikh classmates

- Ask open, respectful questions
- Learn and use names correctly
- Do not assume, and do not touch hair or turbans
- Speak up if someone is being harassed
- Go to an SSA event and bring a friend

## FAQ

### Is it okay to ask a Sikh classmate about their turban?
Usually, yes, if you ask respectfully and out of genuine curiosity. Questions like "What does it mean to you?" tend to go well. Respect it if they would rather not talk about it at that moment.

### Do all Sikhs wear turbans?
No. Some Sikhs wear a turban and keep uncut hair, and some do not. How someone practices is personal. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW]

### What is the best way to support Sikh students on campus?
Learn names, avoid stereotypes, speak up against harassment, and attend events hosted by your Sikh Student Association.

### Can non-Sikhs attend Sikh Student Association events?
Most SSAs welcome everyone. Events like turban-tying days and community meals are often designed for classmates who want to learn.

### What is United Sikh Movement?
United Sikh Movement is a national network that supports 40 Sikh Student Associations with leadership training, mentorship, events, and resources.
