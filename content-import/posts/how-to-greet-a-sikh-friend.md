# How to Greet a Sikh Friend (and Other Questions You Were Afraid to Ask)

You just met your Sikh lab partner's family at move-in. Their grandmother smiles at you, folds her hands, and says something you did not quite catch. You smile back, nod a little too enthusiastically, and spend the rest of the day wondering what you should have said.

We have all been there. Here is a friendly guide to greetings, names, and the small questions classmates are often too shy to ask.

## The short answer

The most common Sikh greeting is "Sat Sri Akal," often said with hands pressed together. You may also hear "Waheguru Ji Ka Khalsa, Waheguru Ji Ki Fateh," especially among more observant Sikhs. With friends your own age, a regular "hi" is completely fine. With elders, saying "Sat Sri Akal" with folded hands is a warm, respectful gesture. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW]

## Common Sikh greetings

### Sat Sri Akal
This is the greeting you are most likely to hear. It is often translated as something like "Truth is the Timeless One." You can say it to anyone, any time of day. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW]

### Waheguru Ji Ka Khalsa, Waheguru Ji Ki Fateh
Often called "the Fateh," this greeting is commonly used among Sikhs, especially in gurdwaras and by Amritdhari Sikhs. It is often translated as "The Khalsa belongs to Waheguru; victory belongs to Waheguru." Our post on the Khalsa gives the background. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW]

If you are not Sikh, "Sat Sri Akal" is the easier and more common choice. If someone greets you with the Fateh, smiling and saying "Sat Sri Akal" back is a kind response. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW]

### Hands together
Many Punjabi and Sikh families greet with palms pressed together, sometimes with a slight bow of the head. You do not have to do it, but it is appreciated, especially with elders. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW]

## How to address Sikh elders

In many Punjabi families, adding **"ji"** after someone's name or title is a sign of respect, like "Auntie ji" or "Uncle ji." Your friend's parents may be happy with "Auntie" and "Uncle," which is common in many South Asian families. When unsure, ask your friend what feels right. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW]

## Names: Singh and Kaur

Many Sikhs use **Singh** (for men) and **Kaur** (for women) as a middle or last name. These names carry meaning in Sikhi and are linked to equality and identity. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW]

A few tips:

- **Do not assume all Singhs are related.** They are usually not.
- **Ask how someone wants to be addressed.** Some people go by their first name, some by a nickname, some by Singh or Kaur.
- **Learn to pronounce first names.** Names like Gurpreet, Harjot, Simran, or Jaskaran are not hard, just unfamiliar to some people. Ask once, practice, and use the name.
- **Many Sikh first names are gender-neutral.** Singh or Kaur often tells you more than the first name. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW]

## Other questions classmates ask

### Can I ask about their turban?
Usually yes, if you ask respectfully. "What does it mean to you?" works well. Never touch someone's turban. See our full guide, "Why Do Sikhs Wear Turbans?"

### Can I invite a Sikh friend to a bar or a barbecue?
Sure. Just do not assume. Some Sikhs do not drink alcohol, use tobacco, or eat meat, as a matter of religious practice, and others make different choices. Having non-alcoholic drinks and a vegetarian option is an easy way to make everyone comfortable. [NEEDS GURSIMAR REVIEW]

### Do I say "Happy Diwali"?
Many Sikhs celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas around the same time. "Happy Bandi Chhor Divas" is thoughtful, or just ask your friend what they celebrate. Our Bandi Chhor Divas guide explains why.

### What if I mess up?
You will be fine. Most Sikhs are happy that you tried. A simple "Sorry, did I say that right?" goes a long way.

## Being a good friend beyond the greeting

- Learn the basics of Sikhi. Our Sikhi 101 guide takes about five minutes.
- Show up to an SSA event: turban-tying days, kirtan nights, and langar-style dinners are usually open to everyone.
- Visit a gurdwara with your friend sometime. Our first-time visitor guide tells you what to expect.
- Speak up if someone mocks your friend's turban or name.
